🖥️ UNIT 1 — Introduction

Objective, Scope and Outcome of the Course  |  Hours: 01

English

Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) is the multidisciplinary field of study concerned with the design, evaluation, and implementation of interactive computing systems for human use, and with the study of the major phenomena surrounding them. It focuses on making the interaction between a human user and a computer system effective, efficient, safe, and satisfying.

Hinglish

HCI ka simple matlab hai — insaan aur computer ke beech ka interaction kaise better banaya jaye. Jab tum kisi app ya website ko use karte ho aur woh "easy to use" lagti hai, toh uske peeche HCI ki research hoti hai. Agar koi app confusing hai, buttons samajh nahi aate, error aata hai bina reason ke — yeh sab HCI ki problems hain jo yeh subject solve karna sikhata hai.

Why HCI Matters Medium

Every piece of software — a banking app, a trading terminal, a college ERP portal, a car's dashboard — is eventually operated by a human. No matter how powerful the backend logic is, if the interface is confusing, users make mistakes, get frustrated, or abandon the product entirely. HCI exists to prevent exactly this by applying scientific principles of psychology, design, and engineering to interface design.

Hinglish

Tum khud ek trading bot/dashboard bana rahe ho — agar uska UI confusing hai (buttons kahan hain, alert kaise dikhega), toh chahe backend kitna bhi accurate ho, user use hi nahi kar payega. Isliye HCI sirf "design" nahi, balki engineering + psychology ka mix hai.

Objective of the Course High

  • To understand the foundations of HCI — what interaction means, and the historical evolution of the field from command-line to GUI to natural/touch interfaces.
  • To study interactive system design concepts, usability principles, and how HCI relates to Software Engineering.
  • To learn GUI design and Aesthetics, along with prototyping techniques used before building the final system.
  • To study model-based design and evaluation approaches (GOMS, KLM, CMN-GOMS, Fitts' Law, Hick-Hyman Law).
  • To understand established guidelines and heuristics (Shneiderman's Eight Golden Rules, Norman's principles, Nielsen's heuristics) for evaluating interfaces.
  • To learn empirical research methods — how to design experiments and analyze usability data scientifically.
  • To study task modelling (HTA, CTT, FSM, Petri Nets) used to formally represent how users perform tasks.
  • To introduce Conceptual Architecture (CA) and Object-Oriented Modeling of user interface design.

The Interaction Loop Low

At its core, every human-computer interaction follows a repeating cycle: the user forms a goal, translates it into an action on the interface, the system executes and responds, and the user perceives and interprets the feedback to decide the next action. This loop is the basis of Donald Norman's "Gulf of Execution" and "Gulf of Evaluation" — covered in later units.

Scope in Industry — Where HCI is Applied Medium

Real-World Examples
  • Banking Apps: Simplifying multi-step fund transfers using clear visual hierarchy and confirmation feedback.
  • Trading Platforms: Real-time price alerts and order panels designed so a trader can act in seconds without misclicking (directly relevant to dashboards like a Traders Panel).
  • E-commerce: Reducing "cart abandonment" by simplifying checkout flow (fewer clicks = better usability).
  • Healthcare Systems: Medical device interfaces designed to prevent life-critical errors (e.g., infusion pump dosage screens).
  • Automotive: Dashboard and infotainment systems designed to minimize driver distraction.

Outcome of the Course High

After completing this course, the student will be able to:

  • CO1: Explain the objective, scope, and historical evolution of HCI, and describe usability concepts.
  • CO2: Apply model-based design techniques (GOMS, KLM, Fitts' Law, Hick-Hyman Law) to evaluate interface efficiency.
  • CO3: Apply established guidelines and heuristics (Shneiderman, Norman, Nielsen) to evaluate and improve interface designs.
  • CO4: Design and conduct empirical usability research including experiment design and statistical analysis (ANOVA).
  • CO5: Model user tasks formally using HTA, CTT, FSM, and Petri Nets.
  • CO6: Understand Conceptual Architecture and apply Object-Oriented Modeling to user interface design.

Clear label + single clear action

This mirrors real interface decisions — e.g. in a trading dashboard, an unlabeled amount field with two ambiguous grey buttons risks a trader misclicking during a fast market move, while a labelled field with one clear, high-contrast action button reduces that risk. This is HCI applied practically.

Key takeaway: HCI is not just "making things pretty." It is a rigorous, evidence-based discipline that combines psychology, design, and engineering to reduce user error and increase efficiency — and it will be evaluated throughout this course using formal models (Fitts' Law, GOMS) and heuristics (Nielsen, Shneiderman), not just subjective opinion.

Unit 1 · Conceptual
Q3. Why is HCI important in modern software development? Explain with an example. ★★★ 5 Marks
5 Marks

A system can be technically correct — fast, bug-free, well-architected — and still fail if users cannot understand how to operate it. HCI ensures the human side of a system is designed with the same rigor as the backend: clear feedback, minimal cognitive load, and error prevention.

Example: A medical infusion pump with a confusing dosage screen can lead to a fatal overdose even though the underlying software logic is correct — this is a classic HCI failure case cited in usability literature, showing why interaction design is treated as a core engineering concern, not an afterthought.

Unit 1 · Conceptual
Q4. Differentiate between traditional Software Engineering and HCI-focused design. ★★ 3 Marks
3 Marks

Traditional Software Engineering focuses on correctness, performance, and architecture, measured by test cases and specification compliance. HCI-focused design focuses on usability, learnability, and satisfaction, measured by task completion time, error rate, and user satisfaction — following a cycle of user research, prototyping, and usability testing rather than a purely linear requirements-to-code process.
