Q1 Describe DVST with diagram.

DVST stands for Direct View Storage Tube. It is a type of raster-less (random scan / vector) CRT-based display device that has the special property of storing the picture information on the screen itself, instead of redrawing (refreshing) the image again and again like a normal CRT.

Hinglish: DVST ek aisi display device hai jisme picture ek baar draw hone ke baad tube ke andar hi "store" ho jaati hai, isliye baar-baar refresh karne ki zaroorat nahi padti — jaise normal CRT me hoti hai.

Construction / Working Principle

A DVST uses two electron guns:

  • Primary gun (writing gun): A high-energy, finely focused electron beam that is used to actually draw / write the picture on the phosphor-coated screen, exactly like a random-scan CRT.
  • Flood gun: A low-energy, wide, diffused beam that continuously floods the entire screen with electrons to keep the stored image visible (refresh the glow), without redrawing the actual lines.

Behind the phosphor-coated screen there is a fine wire mesh called the storage mesh (or storage grid). When the primary (writing) beam strikes the mesh, it leaves behind a pattern of positive charge exactly where the picture was drawn. This charge pattern is what "stores" the image.

The flood gun electrons are of low energy, so normally they cannot penetrate the mesh and reach the phosphor screen. But wherever a positive charge pattern exists (i.e. wherever the picture was drawn), the flood electrons are attracted through the mesh at that spot and continue on to strike the phosphor, causing it to glow. This way the picture keeps glowing continuously without any external refreshing.

Primary gun writes the charge pattern on the storage mesh; flood guns keep it glowing so no refreshing is required.

Advantages

  • No refreshing needed — no flicker even for very complex pictures.
  • Can display very complex images since the refresh rate is not a limitation.
  • Requires no frame buffer / refresh memory, so hardware is comparatively simple.

Disadvantages

  • Selective (partial) erasing of the picture is not possible — to change even a small part, the whole screen must be erased and redrawn.
  • No colour capability (usually monochrome, generally green/orange phosphor).
  • Erasing produces a visible flash and takes time (few hundred milliseconds), so DVST is unsuitable for dynamic/animated graphics.
  • Poor contrast compared to modern raster displays.

Q2 Explain Beam Penetration technique and Shadow Mask technique in CRT colour monitors.

Colour CRT monitors need a way to display multiple colours using electron beams and phosphors. Two classical techniques used for this are the Beam Penetration Method (used with random-scan/vector monitors) and the Shadow Mask Method (used with raster-scan monitors, and this is far more common/popular).

1. Beam Penetration Technique

This method is used mainly in random scan (vector) CRT monitors. The screen is coated with two layers of phosphor — usually red and green:

  • Outer layer (facing the electron gun): Red phosphor
  • Inner layer (deeper inside): Green phosphor

The displayed colour depends on how deep (how far) the electron beam penetrates into the phosphor coating, and this penetration depth depends on the speed/acceleration voltage of the electron beam:

  • Low-speed (low voltage) beam → penetrates only the outer layer → excites only the red phosphor → screen shows red.
  • High-speed (high voltage) beam → penetrates through to the inner layer → excites the green phosphor → screen shows green.
  • Intermediate speeds → excite both layers in varying proportion → produce intermediate colours such as orange and yellow.
Fig: Beam penetration — beam speed decides how deep it penetrates, and hence the resulting colour.

Limitation: Only a limited number of colours (typically 4: red, green, orange, yellow) can be produced, and the colours obtained also depend on the beam's scanning speed, which makes it hard to independently control colour and refresh rate.

2. Shadow Mask Technique

This is the most widely used technique in raster scan colour CRT monitors. It is based on the principle of additive colour mixing using RGB (Red, Green, Blue) phosphor dots.

Construction:

  • The screen has tiny phosphor dots arranged in triads (triangular groups of 3) — one red, one green, one blue dot per triad — called a pixel/triad.
  • Three electron guns are used — one for each colour (R, G, B) — positioned in a triangular (delta) or in-line arrangement.
  • A thin metal plate with tiny holes, called the shadow mask, is placed just behind the phosphor screen, very close to it.

Working: The three electron beams (from R, G, B guns) are aimed so that they converge and pass through the same hole in the shadow mask at the same time. The hole ensures that:

  • The red gun's beam can strike only the red phosphor dot of that triad,
  • The green gun's beam strikes only the green dot,
  • The blue gun's beam strikes only the blue dot.

By varying the intensity of each of the three beams independently, the three dots glow with different brightness, and because the dots are so close together, our eyes blend the red, green and blue light to perceive a single mixed colour at that pixel position. Varying intensities of R, G, B allow a very wide range of colours to be produced (millions of colours in modern versions).

Fig: Shadow mask method — three beams pass through the same mask hole and strike only their matching phosphor dot.

Hinglish: Shadow mask me teen alag guns (R,G,B) ek hi hole se hokar apne apne colour ke dot ko hit karte hain, aur unki intensity change karke bahut saare colours bana sakte hain — yehi aajkal ke colour monitors ka base principle hai.

Q3 What do you mean by Aliasing and Anti-Aliasing?

Aliasing

A computer screen is made up of a finite grid of discrete pixels. When we try to represent a continuous, ideal geometric object (a straight line, circle, or any curve which mathematically has infinite resolution) using this finite pixel grid, we can only approximate it — and this approximation introduces distortion. This distortion/imperfection is called Aliasing.

The most common visible effects of aliasing are:

  • Staircase / jagged effect (jaggies): Lines and edges that are not perfectly horizontal, vertical, or at 45° appear as a series of steps instead of a smooth line.
  • Unequal brightness of lines with different slopes/orientation.
  • Loss of small detail: Thin lines or small objects may partially or completely disappear if they don't align well with pixel positions (this is related to under-sampling, from sampling theory).
  • Moiré patterns in textures/patterns with fine repetitive detail.

Root cause (Sampling Theorem view): Aliasing occurs when a continuous signal (the image) is sampled at a rate lower than twice its highest frequency (violating the Nyquist criterion) — sharp edges in the image correspond to high-frequency components, and the low pixel-sampling rate cannot capture them correctly.

Anti-Aliasing

Anti-aliasing refers to techniques used to reduce or eliminate the distortion (jaggies) caused by aliasing, making lines and edges appear smoother and more natural to the eye.

Main anti-aliasing approaches:

  • Increasing resolution: Using more, smaller pixels reduces the visibility of jaggies (simplest but hardware-costly approach).
  • Unweighted area sampling: A pixel's intensity is set proportional to the percentage of the pixel's area that is covered by the object (line/edge) — pixels that are only partially covered get proportionally dimmer intensity instead of being fully ON or fully OFF.
  • Weighted area sampling: Similar to area sampling, but pixels nearer to the actual line/edge centre are given more weight (more contribution) than pixels farther away, giving an even smoother result.
  • Super-sampling (post-filtering): The image is generated at a much higher resolution internally, and then several samples per final pixel are averaged (filtered) down to produce the final displayed pixel value.
  • Pixel phasing: Shifting the position of a pixel's electron beam slightly toward the correct/ideal position of the line.

Q4 Calculate the points using DDA algorithm — Start (5, 6), End (13, 10)

DDA (Digital Differential Analyzer) is an incremental line-drawing algorithm that calculates points along a line by using the slope of the line.

Step 1: Find ΔX and ΔY

ΔX = X2 − X1 = 13 − 5 = 8 ΔY = Y2 − Y1 = 10 − 6 = 4

Step 2: Find number of steps

Since |ΔX| > |ΔY|, steps = |ΔX|

Steps = max(|ΔX|, |ΔY|) = max(8, 4) = 8

Step 3: Find increments in X and Y

Xinc = ΔX / Steps = 8 / 8 = 1 Yinc = ΔY / Steps = 4 / 8 = 0.5

Step 4: Generate the points

Starting from (X1, Y1) = (5, 6), keep adding Xinc and Yinc, and round off Y to nearest integer at each step:

Q5 Calculate the points using Bresenham's algorithm — Start (20, 10), End (30, 18)

Bresenham's Line Algorithm uses only integer arithmetic (addition, subtraction, and comparison) to decide the next pixel, avoiding the rounding operations required in DDA — making it faster and more efficient.

Step 1: Find ΔX and ΔY

ΔX = X2 − X1 = 30 − 20 = 10 ΔY = Y2 − Y1 = 18 − 10 = 8

Since ΔX > ΔY, X is the driving (decision) axis, i.e. x increments by 1 each step.

Step 2: Calculate initial decision parameter

P0 = 2ΔY − ΔX
P0 = 2(8) − 10 = 16 − 10 = 6

Step 3: Define the two increments

If Pk < 0 : Pk+1 = Pk + 2ΔY (next pixel: x+1, y same) If Pk ≥ 0 : Pk+1 = Pk + 2ΔY − 2ΔX (next pixel: x+1, y+1) 2ΔY = 16 2ΔY−2ΔX = 16 − 20 = −4
