5.1 Introduction to Empirical Research in HCI EXAM HOT

📖 Definition

Empirical Research in HCI is a systematic, evidence-based approach to studying how users interact with computing systems, where conclusions are drawn from observed and measured data (through experiments, surveys, or observations) rather than from opinion, intuition, or authority alone.

🗣 Hinglish Explanation

HCI mein hum sirf "mujhe lagta hai yeh interface accha hai" nahi keh sakte — humein proof chahiye. Empirical research ka matlab hai real users ke sath actual data collect karna (time, errors, satisfaction score, etc.) aur phir us data ko statistically analyse karke decide karna ki konsa design behtar hai. Yeh "guess" nahi, "evidence" par based hota hai.

Why Empirical Research is Central to HCI

  • 🎯 HCI design claims (e.g., "Design A is faster than Design B") need to be testable and falsifiable — empirical methods provide that test.
  • 📊 It converts subjective usability judgments into objective, quantifiable evidence (task time, error rate, satisfaction).
  • 🔁 It follows the scientific method: observation → question → hypothesis → experiment → analysis → conclusion → refinement.
  • 🧪 It underlies usability testing, A/B testing, controlled lab experiments, field studies, and log-data analysis used throughout the HCI lifecycle.

5.2 Motivation for Empirical Research 5-MARK

📖 Definition

The motivation for empirical research in HCI comes from the need to make informed, defensible design decisions instead of relying on designer intuition, and to scientifically demonstrate that one interface, interaction technique, or system genuinely improves human performance or experience over another.

🗣 Hinglish

Socho ek company do login screens design karti hai. Designer bolta hai "mujhe yeh wala zyada accha lagta hai" — lekin yeh sirf opinion hai. Agar hum real users se dono screens use karwaye aur unka time/error measure karein, tab hume fact milta hai ki kaunsi screen better hai. Yahi motivation hai — subjective opinion ko objective evidence mein badalna.

Key Motivating Factors

  • 🧭 Design validation: Confirming whether a new interface/interaction actually improves usability metrics (speed, accuracy, satisfaction).
  • ⚖ Comparing alternatives: Choosing between competing designs (A vs B) with statistical confidence rather than guesswork.
  • 👤 Understanding users: Discovering how real users think, behave, and make errors — which often differs from designer assumptions.
  • 💰 Business/engineering justification: Empirical evidence justifies development cost/time to stakeholders ("Design A reduced task time by 23%, p < 0.05").
  • 🔬 Building HCI theory: Empirical findings accumulate into general models (e.g., Fitts' Law, Hick-Hyman Law) that guide future design.
💡 Example

A company wants to know if a voice-based checkout is faster than a touch-based checkout on their food-delivery app. Instead of assuming, they run a controlled experiment with 30 users, measure completion time, and statistically test whether the difference is significant. This is empirical research driven directly by a real design motivation.

5.3 Issues in Empirical Research MUST KNOW

📖 Definition

Issues in empirical HCI research are the practical, methodological, and ethical challenges that can threaten the accuracy, generalizability, and validity of experimental findings if not carefully controlled.

🗣 Hinglish

Experiment karna itna simple nahi hai jitna dikhta hai. Bahut saari cheezein result ko galat/biased bana sakti hain — jaise participants ka thak jaana, sample size chhota hona, ya researcher ka khud biased ho jaana. In sab issues ko samajhna zaroori hai taaki result trustworthy ho.

Major Issues / Challenges

IssueExplanation
Sampling IssuesSmall or non-representative sample size reduces generalizability of results to the wider user population.
Confounding VariablesUncontrolled extra variables (e.g., time of day, prior experience) may distort the true effect of the independent variable.
Learning / Fatigue EffectsIn within-subject designs, participants may improve due to practice (learning effect) or worsen due to tiredness (fatigue effect).
Ecological ValidityLab settings may not reflect real-world usage context, so results may not transfer to natural environments.
Experimenter BiasResearcher's expectations may unconsciously influence how data is collected or interpreted.
Ethical IssuesInformed consent, privacy of collected data, and avoiding participant harm/stress must be ensured.
Measurement ErrorInaccurate instruments, unclear task instructions, or inconsistent measurement lead to noisy/unreliable data.
Order EffectsThe sequence in which conditions are presented can itself affect performance (mitigated by counterbalancing).
💡
Key Point: Most of these issues are addressed through good experiment design techniques — random assignment, counterbalancing, control groups, and adequate sample size — covered later in this unit.

5.4 Research Question Formulation Techniques EXAM HOT

📖 Definition

A research question is a clear, focused, and answerable question that defines exactly what the empirical study intends to investigate; it drives the choice of variables, experiment design, and statistical analysis used later in the study.

🗣 Hinglish

Research question hi puri study ki neev hai. Agar question hi vague hai ("Kya app accha hai?"), toh experiment bhi vague hoga. Achha research question specific hota hai — "Kya gesture-based navigation, button-based navigation se 20% fast hai new users ke liye?" — isme clearly measurable outcome (time) aur comparison (gesture vs button) diya gaya hai.

Techniques for Formulating a Good Research Question

  1. Start from a real problem/observation: Identify a usability gap, user complaint, or unexplored design alternative.
  2. Narrow the scope: Convert a broad topic ("Is the app usable?") into a specific, testable question ("Does reducing form fields from 8 to 4 reduce checkout abandonment?").
  3. Apply the PICO-style framework (adapted for HCI): Population (who — e.g., novice users), Intervention (what design/technique), Comparison (against what alternative), Outcome (what is measured).
  4. Ensure measurability: The question must map to quantifiable dependent variables (time, errors, SUS score, clicks).
  5. Check feasibility: Question should be answerable within available time, participants, and tools.
  6. State it as a comparison or relationship: Good research questions usually compare two or more conditions or examine a relationship between variables.
💡 Example — Weak vs Strong Research Question

Weak: "Is our website good?" — not measurable, no comparison, too broad.

Strong: "Does a sticky navigation bar reduce average task completion time (in seconds) compared to a traditional top navigation bar, for first-time users completing a search task?" — has population (first-time users), intervention (sticky nav), comparison (traditional nav), and outcome (completion time).

From Research Question → Hypothesis

Typical progression Observation → Research Question → Null Hypothesis (H₀) → Alternative Hypothesis (H₁) → Experiment

5.5 Experiment Design — Basics MUST KNOW

📖 Definition

Experiment design is the systematic plan for how an empirical study will be conducted — specifying the variables, participant groups, tasks, procedure, and controls — so that the effect of one factor on user performance/behaviour can be measured reliably while minimizing bias and confounds.

🗣 Hinglish

Experiment design ka matlab hai poori planning — kaun participants lenge, kya task denge, kaunsi cheez change karenge (independent variable), kaunsi cheez measure karenge (dependent variable), aur kaise bias avoid karenge. Yeh blueprint hai poore experiment ka.

Core Steps in Designing an Experiment

  1. Define the hypothesis derived from the research question.
  2. Identify variables: independent variable(s) (IV), dependent variable(s) (DV), and control variables.
  3. Select participants: determine sample size, recruitment criteria, and how they'll be assigned to conditions.
  4. Choose the design type: between-subjects, within-subjects, or mixed (see Section 5.7).
  5. Design the task(s) and procedure: instructions, training/practice trials, order of conditions (counterbalanced).
  6. Decide measurement methods: timers, error logs, questionnaires (e.g., SUS), eye-tracking, think-aloud.
  7. Pilot test the experiment on a few users to catch design flaws before the real study.
  8. Run the experiment and collect data.
  9. Analyze data statistically (e.g., one-way ANOVA, t-test) and draw conclusions.

5.6 Variables & Hypothesis EXAM HOT

📖 Definition

An Independent Variable (IV) is the factor that the researcher deliberately manipulates or changes (e.g., interface type). A Dependent Variable (DV) is the outcome measured, which is expected to change as a result of the IV (e.g., task completion time). Control variables are kept constant across conditions to prevent them from influencing results.

🗣 Hinglish

Simple yaad rakhne ka tarika: IV = jo tum badalte ho (cause), DV = jo tum measure karte ho (effect). Jaise agar tum test kar rahe ho "font size bada karne se reading speed badhti hai kya" — toh font size IV hai, aur reading speed DV hai. Room lighting, screen brightness jaise cheezein constant rakhni chahiye — woh control variables hain.

Variable TypeMeaningHCI Example
Independent Variable (IV)Manipulated by researcher; the "cause"Navigation type (menu vs gesture)
Dependent Variable (DV)Measured outcome; the "effect"Task completion time, error count
Controlled VariableHeld constant to isolate IV's effectScreen size, lighting, task difficulty
Confounding VariableUncontrolled factor that also affects DV, distorting resultsParticipant's prior app experience

Hypothesis Types

  • Null Hypothesis (H₀): States there is no significant difference/effect between conditions — the default assumption to be tested against.
  • Alternative Hypothesis (H₁ / Hₐ): States that a significant difference/effect does exist.
Example hypothesis pair H₀: μ(gesture) = μ(button) — mean completion time is equal for both navigation types H₁: μ(gesture) ≠ μ(button) — mean completion time differs between navigation types

5.7 Between-Subjects vs Within-Subjects Design 10-MARK FAVOURITE

📖 Definition

In a between-subjects design, each participant experiences only one condition (different groups for each condition). In a within-subjects design, the same participants experience all conditions (repeated measures). A mixed design combines both.

🗣 Hinglish

Between-subjects: alag-alag log alag-alag design try karte hain (Group A sirf design 1, Group B sirf design 2). Within-subjects: same log dono design try karte hain (sabko dono conditions milti hain) — isse participant-to-participant variation kam ho jaati hai, but learning/fatigue effect ka risk aata hai jise counterbalancing se handle karte hain.

Fig 5.2 — Between-subjects (different groups) vs Within-subjects (same group, repeated measures)
AspectBetween-SubjectsWithin-Subjects
Participants per conditionDifferent group for each conditionSame group for all conditions
Participants neededMore (no reuse)Fewer (each person reused)
Individual variabilityHigher noise (different people differ naturally)Lower noise (self-comparison)
RiskGroup differences may confound resultsLearning/fatigue/order effects
Fix for main riskRandom assignment to groupsCounterbalancing order of conditions
Time per participantShorter (one condition only)Longer (all conditions)
💡 Example

Testing 2 keyboard layouts: In a between-subjects version, 15 users type only on Layout A and a different 15 users type only on Layout B. In a within-subjects version, all 15 users type on both Layout A and Layout B (half typing A first, half typing B first — counterbalanced to cancel out learning effect).

5.8 Validity & Reliability 5-MARK

📖 Definition

Internal validity is the degree to which an experiment correctly attributes the observed effect to the manipulated IV (and not to confounds). External validity is the degree to which results generalize beyond the specific experiment (to other users, tasks, or settings). Reliability is the consistency/repeatability of the measurement.

🗣 Hinglish

Internal validity = "Kya result sach mein humari IV ki wajah se hai, na ki kisi aur cheez ki wajah se?" External validity = "Kya yeh result real world mein bhi sach hoga, ya sirf lab mein?" Reliability = "Agar dobara test karein toh same result milega kya?"

ConceptQuestion it answersImproved by
Internal ValidityIs the effect really caused by the IV?Controlling confounds, random assignment
External ValidityDo results generalize to real-world use?Realistic tasks, diverse/representative sample
ReliabilityAre measurements consistent/repeatable?Standardized procedure, precise instruments
⚖
Trade-off: Highly controlled lab experiments tend to have high internal validity but lower external validity (artificial setting); naturalistic field studies have the opposite trade-off.

5.9 Data Analysis — Descriptive vs Inferential Statistics MUST KNOW

📖 Definition

Descriptive statistics summarize and describe the collected data (mean, median, standard deviation). Inferential statistics use sample data to draw conclusions or make generalizations about a larger population, and to test whether observed differences are statistically significant (e.g., t-test, ANOVA, chi-square).

🗣 Hinglish

Descriptive statistics sirf data ko summarize karti hai — jaise "average time 12 seconds tha". Inferential statistics ek step aage jaati hai — yeh batati hai ki "kya yeh difference sirf chance se hua ya sach mein IV ki wajah se hua" — iske liye hypothesis testing (p-value, ANOVA) use hoti hai.

TypePurposeCommon Tools
DescriptiveSummarize/describe sample dataMean, Median, Mode, Std. Deviation, Range, Graphs
InferentialTest hypotheses, generalize to populationt-test, One-Way ANOVA, Chi-square, Regression

Choosing a Statistical Test

  • 🔹 Comparing 2 groups/conditions → t-test
  • 🔹 Comparing 3 or more groups/conditions (one IV) → One-Way ANOVA (this unit's focus)
  • 🔹 Comparing groups with 2 or more IVs → Two-Way ANOVA
  • 🔹 Testing relationship between categorical variables → Chi-square test

5.10 One-Way ANOVA — Theory & Formula EXAM HOT — 10 MARKS

📖 Definition

One-Way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) is an inferential statistical test used to determine whether there is a statistically significant difference between the means of three or more independent groups that differ on a single independent variable (factor), by comparing the variance between group means to the variance within groups.

🗣 Hinglish

ANOVA ka use tab hota hai jab humare paas 3 ya usse zyada groups ho aur hume dekhna ho ki inke averages mein significant difference hai ya nahi. Yeh compare karta hai ki groups ke beech ka difference (between-group variance), groups ke andar ke natural difference (within-group variance) se kitna zyada hai. Agar between-group variance within-group variance se kaafi zyada hai, toh matlab IV ka real effect hai.

Why not just run multiple t-tests?

If we compare 3 groups using separate t-tests (A vs B, B vs C, A vs C), the chance of a false-positive result (Type I error) multiplies with each test. One-way ANOVA tests all groups simultaneously in a single test, controlling this inflated error rate.

Decision Rule

  1. State hypotheses: H₀: μ₁ = μ₂ = μ₃ = ... = μₖ (all group means are equal) vs H₁: at least one group mean differs.
  2. Choose significance level α (commonly 0.05).
  3. Compute F-statistic from sample data (formula above).
  4. Find the critical F-value from F-tables using df₁ = k−1, df₂ = N−k at chosen α.
  5. If calculated F > critical F (or p-value < α) → reject H₀ — at least one group mean is significantly different.
  6. If F ≤ critical F → fail to reject H₀ — no significant difference detected between groups.
  7. If H₀ is rejected, run a post-hoc test (e.g., Tukey's HSD) to find exactly which pairs of groups differ.

5.11 One-Way ANOVA — Worked Numerical Example EXAM HOT — MUST PRACTICE

💡 Problem Statement

Three different menu designs (A, B, C) are tested on users. Task completion time (in seconds) is recorded for 4 users per design. Test at α = 0.05 whether the mean completion time differs significantly across the three menu designs.

Design ADesign BDesign C
101418
121620
91519
131721

Step 1 — Compute Group Means & Grand Mean

x̄A = (10+12+9+13)/4 = 44/4 = 11 x̄B = (14+16+15+17)/4 = 62/4 = 15.5 x̄C = (18+20+19+21)/4 = 78/4 = 19.5 Grand Mean x̄ = (44+62+78) / 12 = 184/12 = 15.33

Step 2 — Between-Groups Sum of Squares (SSB)

SSB = Σ nⱼ(x̄ⱼ − x̄)² = 4(11 − 15.33)² + 4(15.5 − 15.33)² + 4(19.5 − 15.33)² = 4(18.75) + 4(0.0289) + 4(17.39) = 75.0 + 0.12 + 69.56 ≈ 144.68

Step 3 — Within-Groups Sum of Squares (SSW)

Design A deviations from x̄A=11: (−1)²+(1)²+(−2)²+(2)² = 1+1+4+4 = 10 Design B deviations from x̄B=15.5: (−1.5)²+(0.5)²+(−0.5)²+(1.5)² = 2.25+0.25+0.25+2.25 = 5.0 Design C deviations from x̄C=19.5: (−1.5)²+(0.5)²+(−0.5)²+(1.5)² = 2.25+0.25+0.25+2.25 = 5.0 SSW = 10 + 5.0 + 5.0 = 20.0

Step 4 — Degrees of Freedom, MSB, MSW, F

k = 3 groups, N = 12 total observations df₁ (between) = k − 1 = 2 df₂ (within) = N − k = 9 MSB = SSB / df₁ = 144.68 / 2 = 72.34 MSW = SSW / df₂ = 20.0 / 9 = 2.22 F = MSB / MSW = 72.34 / 2.22 ≈ 32.58

Step 6 — Decision

Critical value of F at df₁=2, df₂=9, α=0.05 from F-tables ≈ 4.26.

Since calculated F (32.58) > critical F (4.26), we reject the null hypothesis H₀.

✅
Conclusion: There is a statistically significant difference in mean task completion time among the three menu designs. Since ANOVA only tells us that a difference exists (not where), a post-hoc test (e.g., Tukey's HSD) would be run next to find exactly which pair(s) of designs differ significantly — here, Design A (fastest, mean=11s) clearly outperforms Design C (slowest, mean=19.5s).

📝 Unit 5 — RTU Previous Year & Expected Exam Questions

Q1
What is empirical research? Explain its motivation in the context of HCI. 5 Marks

Empirical research is a systematic, evidence-based method of studying user interaction using measured, observable data rather than opinion. In HCI it is motivated by the need to validate design decisions, compare interface alternatives objectively, understand real user behaviour, justify design choices to stakeholders, and build generalizable HCI theory such as Fitts' Law.

Q2
Discuss the major issues faced while conducting empirical research in HCI. 5 Marks

Key issues include sampling problems (small/unrepresentative samples), confounding variables, learning and fatigue effects in repeated-measures studies, limited ecological validity of lab settings, experimenter bias, ethical concerns around consent and privacy, measurement error, and order effects — most of which are mitigated through careful experiment design such as counterbalancing and control groups.

Q3
Explain techniques for formulating a good research question in an empirical HCI study. 5 Marks

A good research question starts from a real observed problem, is narrowed to a specific and testable form, can be structured using a Population–Intervention–Comparison–Outcome style framework, must map to measurable dependent variables, must be feasible given available time and participants, and is typically phrased as a comparison or relationship between conditions rather than a vague open question.

Q4
Differentiate between independent variable, dependent variable, and confounding variable with examples. 5 Marks

The independent variable is the factor deliberately manipulated by the researcher (e.g., navigation type); the dependent variable is the measured outcome expected to change as a result (e.g., task completion time); a confounding variable is an uncontrolled extraneous factor, such as a participant's prior experience, that also influences the dependent variable and can distort the true effect of the independent variable if not controlled.

Q5
Compare between-subjects and within-subjects experiment designs. When would you prefer one over the other? 10 Marks

In a between-subjects design, different groups of participants each experience only one condition, requiring more participants but avoiding learning and fatigue effects since each person sees only one condition; group differences can, however, confound results, which is mitigated through random assignment. In a within-subjects design, the same participants experience all conditions, which needs fewer participants and reduces individual-variability noise because each person acts as their own control, but introduces risk of learning or fatigue effects, addressed through counterbalancing the order of conditions.

Between-subjects is preferred when conditions might cause lasting learning that cannot be undone (e.g., testing two versions of the same tutorial); within-subjects is preferred when participant availability is limited or when reducing individual variability is important, such as comparing two similar navigation menus.

Q6
What are internal validity and external validity? How do they trade off against each other? 5 Marks

Internal validity is the degree to which an observed effect can correctly be attributed to the manipulated independent variable rather than confounds, improved through controlling confounds and random assignment. External validity is the degree to which the results generalize beyond the specific experiment to real-world users, tasks, and settings, improved through realistic tasks and representative samples. There is typically a trade-off: tightly controlled lab experiments maximize internal validity but sacrifice external validity, while naturalistic field studies maximize external validity but sacrifice control and hence internal validity.

Q7
Explain One-Way ANOVA. Derive the formula for the F-statistic and explain the decision rule. 10 Marks

One-way ANOVA is an inferential statistical test that determines whether the means of three or more independent groups, differing on a single factor, are significantly different, by comparing between-group variance to within-group variance. Total variation (SST) is split into between-groups variation (SSB, how far each group mean is from the grand mean) and within-groups variation (SSW, how far individual scores are from their own group mean), such that SST = SSB + SSW. Dividing each sum of squares by its degrees of freedom (df₁ = k−1 for between, df₂ = N−k for within) gives the mean squares MSB and MSW, and the F-statistic is F = MSB / MSW.

Decision rule: state H₀ (all group means equal) and H₁ (at least one differs), compute F from sample data, compare it to the critical F value from F-tables at the chosen significance level (commonly α=0.05) with (df₁, df₂); if calculated F exceeds the critical F (or p < α), H₀ is rejected, indicating a statistically significant difference exists among group means, and post-hoc tests can then identify exactly which groups differ.

Q8
Three interface designs are tested with 4 users each; completion times are Design A: 10,12,9,13; Design B: 14,16,15,17; Design C: 18,20,19,21. Perform a one-way ANOVA at α=0.05 and state your conclusion. 10 Marks

Group means: x̄A=11, x̄B=15.5, x̄C=19.5; grand mean x̄=15.33. SSB ≈ 144.68 with df₁=2; SSW = 20.0 with df₂=9. This gives MSB=72.34, MSW=2.22, and F = MSB/MSW ≈ 32.58. The critical value of F at df₁=2, df₂=9, α=0.05 is approximately 4.26. Since the calculated F (32.58) far exceeds the critical F (4.26), the null hypothesis is rejected — the mean completion times differ significantly across the three designs, with Design A performing fastest and Design C slowest.

Q9
Differentiate between descriptive and inferential statistics with suitable examples from HCI experiments. 5 Marks

Descriptive statistics summarize and describe the collected sample data using measures such as mean, median, mode, and standard deviation — for example, reporting that the average task completion time was 12.4 seconds. Inferential statistics go further by using sample data to test hypotheses and draw generalizable conclusions about the wider user population, using tests such as the t-test or one-way ANOVA — for example, determining whether a 2-second difference in completion time between two interface designs is statistically significant or merely due to chance.

Q10
Why is one-way ANOVA preferred over running multiple t-tests when comparing more than two groups? 5 Marks

Running separate t-tests for every pair among three or more groups multiplies the chance of a false-positive (Type I) error, since each individual test carries its own error probability and these compound across multiple comparisons. One-way ANOVA instead tests all groups simultaneously within a single statistical test, controlling the overall Type I error rate at the chosen significance level while still determining whether at least one group mean differs significantly from the others.
